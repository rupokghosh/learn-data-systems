Maintainability

Most of the cost of software is not in building it. It is in keeping it running, fixing it, and changing it for years afterwards, usually by people who did not write it.

Three design principles help keep a system workable over its lifetime.

1. Operability: make life easy for operations

Good operations can often work around bad software, but not the other way round. Help the people running the system with clear monitoring, predictable behaviour, good documentation, automation that can be overridden by hand, sensible defaults, and no reliance on any single machine.

2. Simplicity: manage complexity

Large projects tend to sink into a "big ball of mud": tangled dependencies, special cases, inconsistent names. Some complexity is essential because the problem itself is hard. Much of it is accidental: it comes only from how the system was built. The best tool for removing accidental complexity is a good abstraction, a clean interface that hides messy details.

3. Evolvability: make change easy

Requirements always change: new features, new regulations, new scale, new platforms. A simple, well-abstracted system is much easier to change than a tangled one. Evolvability is really simplicity and good abstractions, seen over time.
